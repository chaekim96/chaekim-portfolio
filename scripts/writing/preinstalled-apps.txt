**What’s the Point of Preinstalled Apps?**

I recently had an assignment where I needed to record a video using a teleprompter.

I figured this would be easy. Download a teleprompter app, paste in my script, record, done.

Instead, I spent more time than I expected trying to find a decent one.

Some apps locked basic features behind subscriptions. Others were free but clunky. A few had way more functionality than I needed.

All I wanted was a box for my script, adjustable scrolling speed, readable text, and a clean interface.

So I stopped looking and made one.

I opened Claude Code, told Opus what I wanted, and within a few minutes I had a working teleprompter.

The first version worked, but the UI was pretty bad.

So I told it to take another stab at the design.

The second version surprised me.

It wasn't just better than the first. I preferred it to the teleprompter apps I had downloaded. It was simple, did exactly what I wanted, and somehow felt like it belonged on my computer.

And that's when I had a thought:

**What's the point of preinstalled apps?**

**Why do our computers come with apps?**

For most of computing history, the answer has been pretty obvious.

Software is expensive to build.

When you buy a new computer or phone, the manufacturer gives you a collection of tools that cover the things you're probably going to need.

Calculator. Notes. Calendar. Weather. Mail. Voice recorder. Browser.

Then we customize from there.

We delete some apps, move others around, and download the things we actually need. Eventually, everyone's home screen or dock looks completely different.

But generative AI introduces another possibility.

**What if instead of customizing which apps we have, we generate the software we need?**

Imagine buying a new iPhone.

Instead of being greeted by pages of preinstalled apps, you're greeted by Siri.

You say:

"Make me a simple expense tracker for my trip to Japan."

A small interface appears.

"Give me a timer that alternates between 45 seconds of exercise and 15 seconds of rest."

Done.

"Make me a teleprompter. I want to paste in text, adjust the scrolling speed, and control it with keyboard shortcuts."

Done.

Maybe you use that tool once and it disappears.

Maybe you keep using it and it becomes a permanent icon on your home screen.

The operating system slowly becomes yours.

## From apps to intent

We've spent decades adapting ourselves to software.

Someone else decides what features an app should have, where the buttons go, what the workflows look like, and increasingly, what those features should cost per month.

We then choose whichever application comes closest to what we want.

AI could flip that relationship.

Instead of searching:

**"What's the best app for X?"**

You say:

**"I need X."**

And the computer figures out the rest.

That's a much bigger change than making app development cheaper.

It changes the fundamental unit of computing.

Today, that unit is the **application**.

Tomorrow, it might be the **intent**.

## What does Apple look like in that world?

This is where things get interesting.

Apple spends enormous resources designing, building, testing, updating, and maintaining software across its ecosystem.

Obviously, much of that isn't going anywhere.

I don't want my iPhone generating a new version of Settings every morning. I probably don't want an AI improvising how Messages works. Camera, security, payments, communications, and other core systems need reliability and consistency.

But does Apple really need to build every utility?

Does every function need its own predetermined interface?

And does every new capability need to become another app?

Maybe the operating system of the future looks less like a collection of software and more like a collection of primitives: camera, location, payments, storage, messaging, identity, sensors, and compute.

The agent sits on top of them.

You tell it what you're trying to accomplish, and it assembles those capabilities into whatever interface you need.

In that world, Siri isn't another application.

**Siri becomes the operating system.**

That could have enormous implications for companies like Apple.

Instead of continually expanding the number of applications they build and maintain, they could invest more heavily in the underlying platform and agent that generates experiences on top of it.

Software development doesn't disappear. It moves down a layer.

Apple builds the trusted infrastructure.

AI builds the last mile.

## And then there's the App Store

This is where my little teleprompter experiment becomes more uncomfortable.

Because the question isn't really whether Apple needs Calculator.

It's whether I need to download a calculator app at all.

The App Store was built around scarcity.

Building software required technical knowledge, time, and money. Developers built applications because users couldn't easily build those applications themselves.

Distribution became valuable because creation was difficult.

But what happens when creation becomes trivial?

There is an enormous category of apps that aren't particularly complicated. Timers. Trackers. Calculators. Converters. Simple editors. Scanners. Dashboards. Journals. Teleprompters.

They're useful, but many are essentially interfaces wrapped around a handful of functions.

If I can describe exactly what I want and have a functional version generated in minutes, the economics of that software start looking very different.

Why search through 20 teleprompter apps?

Why read reviews?

Why compare subscription plans?

Why pay $4.99 a month?

Just make one.

## Not everything is bloatware

My first reaction after building the teleprompter was:

"Everything is just bloatware at this point."

I don't actually think that's right.

Good software does much more than provide an interface. Great products encode years of expertise, edge cases, infrastructure, network effects, trust, and thoughtful product decisions.

I'm not generating Photoshop this afternoon.

I'm not vibe-coding my own bank.

And I probably shouldn't generate my own password manager.

But the threshold for what deserves to be an application is moving.

Fast.

There is now a growing category of software where searching, downloading, onboarding, and paying for an application may actually require more effort than creating exactly what you need.

That's new.

And I think we're still underestimating what it means.

Because when I needed that teleprompter, I didn't consciously decide to participate in some new paradigm of computing.

I was just annoyed.

I needed software.

I couldn't find software I liked.

So I made it.

A few years ago, that sentence would have implied that I knew how to build an app.

Today, increasingly, it just means I knew what I wanted.

And if that becomes normal, the interesting question isn't:

**What's the point of preinstalled apps?**

It's:

**What's the point of apps?**
