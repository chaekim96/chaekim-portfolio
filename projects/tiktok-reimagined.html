The short version.

The problemPeople search TikTok for proof, then leave to get the answer.

About half of U.S. consumers have used TikTok as a search engine, but almost none prefer it to Google. The answer is in the videos, spread across a dozen clips.

What I'd ship firstAn answer card on top of search, in three categories.

Beauty, food, and travel, where people want to see it work. Every line links to the creator and the second of video that backs it up. No ads in it at launch.

How I'd measure itFewer searches again, more taps on sources.

Did people stop searching the same thing again? Did they tap a source, follow a creator, or save? Answers people trust send viewers to creators instead of away from them.

Why it fits TikTokIt turns search into the brand story advertisers want.

Search Hubs, Pulse Mentions, and Symphony already exist. Answers tie them into one pitch: be the answer when people ask, not just the ad they scroll past.

The rule that holds it together: the answer is never for sale. Brands can pay to appear next to it, never inside it.

Part 1 · The thought piece

Why TikTok search should answer.

For most of the past year, I worked on a question brands didn't use to ask.

When someone asks ChatGPT about your category, does it mention you? That was the work at Lucent, an AI search visibility platform. Brands used to fight for the top blue link. Now they want to be the sentence an AI says out loud.

The same shift is happening inside TikTok, and it rarely gets called search. Type "sunscreen for oily skin under $20" and you get a grid of videos. The answer is in there somewhere. It's spread over a dozen clips, each forty seconds long, some of them ads, and you have to watch them all to piece it together.

TikTok has something no search engine has: real people showing the product working on camera. It just hasn't turned that proof into an answer.

"TikTok,
just tell me."

People already search here. They just don't stay.

Adobe's 2026 consumer survey found that 49% of U.S. consumers have used TikTok as a search engine, up from 41% in 2024. But only 4% of Gen Z said they prefer it to Google, down from 8%, and 14% of consumers said they're more likely to rely on ChatGPT than Google.1

That gap is the opportunity. People come to TikTok to see it, then go somewhere else to decide. The search habit is there. The answer isn't.

Where it starts: one answer card.

Search gets a new top result: a short answer composed from creator videos. Every line carries a number, and every number opens the creator's video at the second that backs it up. If the videos disagree, the answer says so.

I'd launch in three categories where people want proof before they buy: beauty, food, and travel. No ads inside the card, and no answer at all when there aren't enough good videos. A short list of honest answers beats a long list of weak ones.

I'd judge it on three numbers: how often people stop searching the same thing again, how often they tap a source, and what they do next (follow, save, or shop). If people read the answer and leave, it's taking views from creators. If they tap through, it's sending viewers to them.

Creators become the sources.

None of this works if creators feel mined. So it's opt-in, and being cited has to pay. A cited creator gets a "Source" badge on the clip, the views that come from the answer, and a share of the revenue from searches their videos answered. Creators can see which of their clips were cited and for what questions, which is a better content brief than any trend report.

Brands supply facts. Creators supply proof.

Today a brand can buy a Search Hub, a branded page at the top of search results, announced at TikTok World in May 2026.2 It can run Pulse Mentions, which places ads next to posts where people are already talking about the brand or its category.3 Both put the brand near the answer. Neither lets the brand make sure the answer is right.

So brands get an Answer Brief in Ads Manager: approved facts like price, ingredients, and where to buy, plus claims they can't make. TikTok never writes a brand claim that the brand didn't approve or a creator didn't show. The brand's facts fix the details. The creators' videos carry the persuasion.

How it makes money.

A few ways it could pay, in the order I'd try them:

  • Search Hubs as "go deeper." The answer links to the brand's Search Hub when that brand comes up. It's already sold, so it's the easiest yes for advertisers.
  • Sponsored slot under the answer. A clearly labeled brand card below the answer, priced like search ads. Real money, as long as it never looks like part of the answer.
  • Shop from the answer. When the answer names a product, checkout through TikTok Shop. Commission revenue, but it should wait until people trust the answers.

I'd lead with Search Hubs. It turns an existing product into a better one, and it keeps the answer itself clean, which is the whole asset.

The pitch to advertisers.

Most brands still think of TikTok as the top of the funnel: you go there to get noticed, and people buy somewhere else. Answers changes the story from "reach people" to "be the answer when they ask." If I were positioning it, the statement would be:

For brands whose customers want to see it work before they buy, TikTok Answers is the only search result built from real people showing the product. Unlike AI search that summarizes web pages, every answer links to the video that proves it.

And one message for each side of the market:

ViewersAsk once. See who said it.
CreatorsYour best video becomes the answer, and you get paid when it is.
BrandsMake sure the facts are right. Let creators make the case.

Can TikTok actually do this?

Most of the pieces exist. TikTok already sells Search Ads and Search Hubs, makes ads with its Symphony AI tools, and captions videos automatically, so it knows what's said in each clip. It has labeled AI-made content with C2PA Content Credentials since 2024.4 The answer card is a new layer on top of those.

The hard part isn't the technology. It's accuracy and trust: knowing when not to answer, and keeping paid and unpaid clearly apart.

Questions I'd expect.

If I were presenting this at TikTok, these are the questions I'd expect in the room, and how I'd answer them.

What happens when the answer is wrong?

Every line is tied to a source, so a wrong line can be traced and pulled. Health, money, and safety questions get no composed answer at launch, only source videos. And viewers can flag a line, which sends it back for review.

Won't this take views away from creators?

That's the number I'd watch most closely. The design pushes people into videos: the answer is short, and every claim is a tap away from the clip. If source taps don't hold up, the answer is too long.

Won't brands just buy the answer?

No. That's the one rule. Brands can pay to appear next to the answer and to make sure their facts are right, never to change what creators said or which videos get cited.

Isn't this just Google's AI Overviews?

AI Overviews summarize web pages. This summarizes people showing the thing on camera, and every claim opens the video. That's the part no one else can copy.

What about AI-generated videos as sources?

AI-labeled videos aren't eligible as sources at launch. If the proof is the point, the proof has to be real.

How does this work with TikTok's U.S. setup?

TikTok's U.S. joint venture, formed in January 2026, retrains the recommendation system on U.S. data under Oracle's oversight.5 An answer layer would need to be built and reviewed inside that same setup, so I'd launch it in the U.S. only after that review, and possibly in other markets first.
